# Idea Brief Template

[Lifecycle and Folders](001-lifecycle-and-folders.md) defines an idea as a two-pager: what the problem is, why it might
be worth solving, roughly what solving it would involve, and what would make it not worth doing. This template is the
section order that carries those answers. A brief argues that a problem is worth solving; it does not design the
solution.

## Sections

1. **Title and one-line summary** — one sentence a stranger understands, telling a reader whether to continue. A
   provenance line follows: `> Idea, added YYYY-MM-DD.` or `> Surfaced YYYY-MM-DD while executing <plan-slug>.`
2. **Problem and context** — one specific example of the status quo failing, grounded in counts, sizes, or measurements
   where they exist. A measured problem can be groomed; a general complaint cannot.
3. **Why now** — the urgency, dependency, or window that makes this timely rather than eventual.
4. **Prior art** — two to five named precedents, each with a link and a clause: a tool, a pattern, a standard, or an
   earlier plan. Capture stays lightweight, and the deeper study happens at promotion. No prior art on a substantial
   idea means nobody looked.
5. **Proposed direction** — at most three elements, sketched at a level a reader grasps at once. No file paths,
   signatures, wireframes, or acceptance criteria: that detail is the plan's.
6. **Scope and non-goals** — what is in, and an explicit list of what a reader would reasonably expect but is
   deliberately left out.
7. **Risks and open questions** — the rabbit holes worth flagging now, and the unknowns that block promotion. None at
   all means the idea is either over-specified or under-thought.
8. **Success and promotion signal** — what would make pursuing it worthwhile, and what ready for `backlog/` means for
   this idea. State an observable fact, a cited and dated figure, or a labelled judgement, never an invented metric.

Sections 1 to 3 say what the problem is and why it is worth solving. Sections 4 to 6 say what solving it involves.
Sections 7 and 8 say what would make it not worth doing: the risks that could sink it, and the signal whose absence
means it should not be promoted.

## Why This Shape

Each section blocks a known failure. A summary a stranger cannot read wastes every future grooming pass. A problem with
no concrete example cannot be judged real by [Ideas Grooming](../../../workflows/plan/plan-ideas-grooming.md). A brief
with no prior art invites reinvention. A direction that names file paths has started planning before anyone decided to
plan.

## Two Pages Is the Point

A brief that needs five pages is a plan in a brief's clothes. Either request the plan and write its six documents, or
cut the brief until its argument stands alone.

Brevity keeps briefs cheap to retire, and retiring most of them is the useful outcome of grooming.
